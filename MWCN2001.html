Mobile and Wireless Communication Networks, (MWCN 2001)

Gavoa Praia, Brazil

Attracted by a compact workshop like conference, with some interesting people at an unusual location, I accepted to present a paper around the RadioSphere plan. The title, of "mobile and networks", indicated an opening for the systems oriented approach I find most interesting. Besides, I did not attend SigMobile in Rome in July, since I had more important things at home at that time.

Someone who was in Rome, in the organizing committee, but also came to Gavoa was Christopher Rose of Winlab, who also gave a kind of keynote speech over a whole afternoon. Professor Rose is a radio guy, so I have now, together with several other presentations here, started to understand much more about this piece in the mobile puzzle. CDMA, OFDM, waterfilling, far-near problem, rake-receivers, multi-path propagation interference and in general signal processing has not been my area of specialty, but having read some books and listened to a by now large number of presentations I feel I can orient myself. It's fun.

Rose viewed his role as to provoke further thought and presented some drastically new ideas, mainly around the consequences of short-range but very high speed radio transmissions. He calls his idea "infostations", which should utilize extreme bandwidths reaching up to gigabit/s transfers as you quickly pass through, exchanging your accumulated need for data until you step into another infostation. Part of the idea is also the ubiquitous availability of large inexpensive small storage, and that you may exchange data more or less automatically with other data-strollers. A kind of ad-hoc walknet, where part of the transfer happens by people physically moving around.

Chris Rose is engaging and fun to hear, but is very much of radio and less of the complete systems architect. It is easy to see from the equations that short range makes wonder for radio transfer rates. However, data also has to come to the infostation in some way. I asked about this, and he happily answered "Well, that's a wired network issue, and there bandwidth is infinite" :-).

In general I still feel that "mobility" has far too much emphasis on radio. It is an important but far from dominating component in the mobile system. Problem is that the business is so decomposed into narrow sub-areas, that few people can see the complete system in its correct light and proportions. The whole conference suffered from this, in that it emphasized the radio access far too much, and had several sessions on CDMA and a whole session on frequency and capacity planning. Two sessions, (6 papers), on Quality of Service were also there, but mostly limited to the radio interface. One paper discussed admission control schemes in the context of new calls, handover of existing ones, and one lifted the issue to network level in "Using DiffServ in 3G Cellular Networks".

I tried to change this in my presentation, and also surpass Chris Rose in visionary outlook, which I started with saying: "While this paper is placed in a session called 3G Networks, it is about the 4:th generation of such systems. And I must tell you, that the fourth generation mobile system is not going to be an air interface". And yes, that is the case of the future. A mobile network that is a direct extension of the Internet, not just technically but architecturally, culturally and philosophically. The radio access interface is not the decisive component, but a piece that could be added, by anyone, and replaced, at any point in the network. Just as we add, remove and replace computers today, usually through ethernet interfaces. I improvised around the long list of issues involved in RadioSphere:

  1. Authentication. For at least two reasons. PKI needed.
  2. Encryption and Privacy. Can be arbitrarily sophisticated. Care to be tracked?
  3. Customer and group membership management. Light-weight.
  4. Electronic economic transactions. In case you have to pay for access it should be electronic.
  5. The Business model. Less money for bit transport, anyway.
  6. User Interface technology for mobile devices. You must have it with you.
  7. Mobility of IP-addresses. MobileIP and associated questions.Triangle routing is NOT an option. Multicasting.
  8. Adaptive and self-regulating cell-coverage. Necessary for consumer electronics scenario.
  9. Mobile Subnetworks. One Foreign Agent for the whole ship?
  10. Advanced Applications. The world beyond the web. And the phone. (some here)
  11. Location-aware applications. Among few genuinely mobility oriented services.
  12. Integration with broadcast technologies and DVB-T in particular. (more here)
  13. Application Architectures for Resource Limited Small Computers. Such as ASP-style.
  14. Service discovery. Where is a Printer? A CD-Burner?
  15. Context mobility. Keeping all state, security associations, open files,etc, while moving.
  16. Ad-hoc networking, peer-to-peer and Multicast optimization. Including walknet.
  17. Quality of Service in Mobile and IP networks. Maintaining reservations while path changes.
  18. Handover management. In the context of packets, small cells and maintained QoS.
  19. Mobile IP telephony. Header compression and QoS.
  20. Smart antennas. Mobile networks can carry them.
  21. Short range gigabit/s radio. Some call it "Infostations".
  22. Ipv6. Is it needed? Must Mobile IPv6 be deployed at same time? Expect lots of unresolved issues.
  23. Interworking with UMTS. OK, you can attach a UMTS basestation to the RadioSphere.
  24. Legal Issues. Can lawyers sabotage bandwidth sharing?. Is FCC/Government regulation needed?

Yes, this is material for weeks of talking, so I had to be brief. Since I was in Brazil I couldn't resist being a bit exotic and mention Arena/IT-Hockey :-), and discuss around its implications for communications and application architectures.

My approach to things is a bit confusing to many people, who are used to limited and narrow elaborations on a single issue. What box to put this in? Help! It was however received very well and professor Djamel who was the organizing chair smiled. In any case, as people who know me already understand: any attempt to put me in a box or "allocate" me to a particular limited area will be rejected.

Another person who also, later in his presentation, referred to my speech, was Mobile IP "guru" Charles Perkins, who talked two times about various aspects about Mobile IPv6. As he is the main author of the IETF RFC on Mobile IPv6 I listened carefully. I cannot say that I learned anything really knew - I knew most of the material on beforehand, but I got confirmed a number of intuitions, concerns and even added some new perspectives. I think I know better now how this should be done.What worries is maybe that the undertaking grows a bit, since I see that IPv6 introduction is hard to avoid. While this is good, it makes things bigger.

Funny that I had to go all the way to Brazil to establish a good contact with Center for Wireless Communication, CWC), at the University of Oulu. The manager of CWC is Matti Latva-Aho, a real hard core radio guy. He made his PhD in "Advanced receivers for wideband CDMA systems" and has continued that track. He is good and knows what he is talking about, (while I am not the one to judge). It was anyway rewarding to listen to him.

Nevertheless, he understands the power of the systems view I presented around RadioSphere, and after hours of informal discussions, we understand each other and have decided that a joint meeting should take place as soon as we can find suitable time. An urgent goal is to see if an EU project around RadioSphere can be constructed for the 16 October 5:th Framework deadline.

"- And now, a word from our sponsors". The Industry panel consisted, (from left), of managers of Brazil branches of MicroSoft, Siemens and Nokia. They all made well prepared and nice presentations, Siemens and Nokia especially talking about 3G. Mostly from a user and market perspective. Yes I like the services envisioned, but in my comment speech to the panel I could just ask them to reflect over the huge price/performance differences between the wired Internet as we know it, and the wireless/mobile one as envisioned by 3G, and point out the alternative way of doing things which may solve the problem. This way, however, means that mobile operators may almost disappear. Operators in general will be very important, (a revenge for the wired backbone), and even rich, but those who build their revenue and large organization mainly on charging insanely high for that final wireless packet hop, those will have a bleak future. This is inherent in the goal of evening out the price/performance difference between wired and wireless. This is inherent in reaching the enormous advantage of being able to use the same applications and sites for both wired and unwired access. However, MicroSoft, Siemens and Nokia have nothing to fear long-term, they just gain because the market can quickly be a mass-market.

The industry panelists showed some quite fun 3G-commercials. In true Latino-Macho style they all had as the theme "using 3G gadgets to impress on beautiful women". I started my comment on admitting that now I'm almost a 3G fan. The Nokia representative, a French-Brazilian woman who had worked for Nokia in Paris for 10 years, told however a story about her Finnish colleagues who always brought their fancy mobile phones to restaurants and called each other to attract attention and brag. It doesn't work nowadays, she said, and claimed that 95% of Finnish teenagers have a mobile phone.

Anyway, Matti liked my discussion so much that he walked 3 kilometers along the beach and bought a beer for me from the locals. Initially being a bit disappointed by my preference for Coca Cola, he cheered up after realizing that he could have it himself.

Brazil

Brazil is a country of extreme differences. While there is certainly great wealth and modern industry in the southwest around burgeoning Sao Paolo, there is so much poverty and lack of progress in the poverty-stricken old northeast and the isolated and huge amazonas. The differences in wealth are really disturbing and unproductive for society as a whole. Poor people sell their labour for almost nothing, and are used just to walk dogs and open garage doors when the wealthy people of Ipanema want to drive their BMW down Avenida do Atlantica. The conference was held 60 kilometers outside Recife in the poor north-east. Since business is in the southwest around Sao Paolo and Rio de Janeiro all flights go through those cities, and I had the opportunity to visit Rio for a few days.

Brazil has 160 million people, of a mixed European, African and native Indian heritage. They have little racial problems, they mix happily, but have lots of other troubles. 60 million people lack decent housing and live in places like the infamous Favelas in Rio. Climbing the inconvenient heights are shacks housing millions, often without water, sanities or electricity. An unbelievable 12 million children are said to be abandoned and homeless. The problem is much one of education, since that is the key to give people the tools necessary to guide themselves in a complex world and be able to do productive things. Without it, there is no chance but to resort to criminality or lousily paid jobs, selling almost anything on the beach and earning almost nothing. Or, moving to Sao Paolo with a small hope for a job in a truck factory. While improvements are going on it seems to be so much work. Still it seems like private schools, afforded only by the rich, are much much better, since almost all university students are recruited from those. I walked through the little neighbouring poor village of Nova Cruz and stood by the "Municipality School" and thought for a while, looking at the kids.... Do they have the motivation, background, and outlook, and orientation of the world and the life for knowing why English, second order equations, modular arithmetic's and biotechnology is important? Hard, very hard, if their parents have no idea, and no one is there to give them the knowledge-praising ideals that I luckily was given as a child.

Brazil has a long history as the land of the future, which never seems to come, with lots of brave attempts and grand projects. President Kubitcheks visionary plan for developing the interior of Brazil included the establishment of a new capital, Brasilia. Since most people live along the coast, the rest of this huge land, (larger than USA excluding Alaska), is little exploited. Brasilia was built from scratch 1000 kilometers inland in the early 60's and is now home to almost 2 million people. Famous architect Oscar Niemayer designed it, and has become a classic in architecture history. For good and bad. Other grand projects were the transamazonas highway through thousands of kilometers of unfriendly wilderness. Anyway, large debts built up, currency inflated, and in the traditional South-American style there was much corruption. 11 out of 25 presidents have been forced to go before formal term is over. As late as 1994 playboy-president Collor was forced from office being accused of transferring a billion dollar to own accounts. Surprisingly enough, however, the following presidents, such as Henrique Cardoso, a previous sociology professor from Sao Paolo, proved honest and managed to get Brazil on a good track. The so called Plano Real stabilized the currency, and foreign investments in Brazil have reached record levels in late 90's.

Brazil also has a fascinating colonial history of brave Portuguese explorers who sailed to unknown coasts and the tough "Bandeirantes" who investigated the vast interior and extended Portuguese Brazil far beyond the original Treaty of Tortedilha. They fought the French and the Dutch who wanted pieces of Brazil, built sugar plantations, growed coffee, benefitted by selling natural rubber from the amazonas to the emerging car-industry and built an opera-house deep inside the amazonas jungle in Manaus. Beautiful colonial towns are still there, such as Olinda and original capital Salvador de Bahia in the north-east, and the town-jewels from the gold mining boom in Minas Gerais, such as Ouru Preto and Tiradentes. Brazil, by the way, is still one of the worlds main mining countries, and the whole mountain-sides of iron ore are rusting, major competitors to LKAB.

Looking down at the city - this area is called Flamengo - and the Sugar Loaf, Copacabana hiding behind the mountain in the right of the picture, Ipanema further to the right, I get to think about Deirdres Samba:

"Mellan Praia de Flamengo och det fagra Ipanema,
finns de rikas heta stränder,
men de fattiga i Rio,
bor högt över alla andra,
högsta berget bor jag på,
vinden svalkar, solen bränns,
där finns sorg som inte känns."

Cornelis Vreeswijk

 

Football and Jungle-Hockey

Flamengo's kids are practicing on the beach. In the sand. Without shoes. You get hard feet and strong ankles. Everybody does it. It is Brazilian culture. Beaches and football is a way of life.

Of course I had to visit Maracana, the world largest football stadium. The Brazilians are so very proud of having been able to beat Sweden once in a world championship final, in 1958 it was I think, that they have dedicated a whole monument to it. Oh yes I remember my childhood, sitting there north of the arctic circle studying the book and the photos about 1958 World Championship. Pele, Garrincha and Mazzola. And probably the best Swedish team ever.

While being and old football-player, (Norrbotten junior champion 1974, with several later national team players in the team), my purpose was to introduce some hockey-culture in this country. First, the training camp up by the jungle-border. It was really remote and lonely.
Then, I went down to Copacabana to show the kids a cool new sports. The customs had never seen hockey-sticks, so they confiscated them just to be on the safe side. Therefore I had to just illustrate the basic movements and try to explain that ice is not just those cubes you put in the drinks. Anyway, after a few rounds I think hockey may have a great future in Brazil!
