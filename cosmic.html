Cosmic
Communications

Write, don't radiate

How will first contact happen — by radio, or by artifact? Strangely enough, we think artifacts are more likely.

It is often many, many orders of magnitude cheaper, in energy, to write a message down in some medium and literally throw it at the recipient than to radiate it electromagnetically — provided you can tolerate delay beyond light transit time. So perhaps SETI should be sifting through dust, pebbles and rocks as well as sweeping the spectrum.

Nature's cover blurb

What the editors made of it

It's almost a given that remote civilizations will try to contact us using radio waves. The search for extraterrestrial intelligence was first mooted by Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison 45 years ago, and since then two million years of CPU time has been given to SETI@home in the spirit of that paper: the probability of success is difficult to estimate, but if we never search, the chance of success is zero. Christopher Rose and Gregory Wright propose a new take. It seems the "Sounds of Earth" gold disks aboard Voyagers 1 and 2 were on the right track. The message-in-a-bottle idea of sending physical objects across space is highly energy efficient, and we should search for artefacts in the Solar System now.

If that blurb made little sense, some very good science writers put it better — see the press coverage. A few even managed to drag the right words out of me during radio interviews, at least where they could contain my digressions and technospeak.

The argument

Why matter wins

This work was an accidental outgrowth of research on terrestrial wireless. It is careful about the energetics, and it deliberately stacks the deck in favour of radiation — assuming things like infinite bandwidth — before deriving outer bounds on the ratio of energy needed to deliver a message by inscribed matter versus by radiation.

We worry about directed radiation, aperture sizes, and the fact that there are many targets to reach. Matter transmission still wins.

Later versions added the delivery problem (how do you slow the particle down at the destination?), the construction problem (how much energy to build the particle in the first place?), and a version of the civilization communication problem — civilizations are almost certainly asynchronous, whether through celestial calamity like meteor strikes or through competition and stupidity. We had wanted to hold these back, but various referees felt they were necessary to convince readers that evidence of ET might turn up in grains of sand, or on comets and meteors.

We also considered the ballistic aiming problem, since gravitational perturbations might knock a package off course. The effect seems small.

Of course, if energy is no object — consider Freeman Dyson's sun-herding, or building what amounts to a giant blinker around a star — our energy comparisons are irrelevant. But since such feats are not presently on our technical horizon, we are biased toward worrying about energy.

Quantitative analogies

How big is "many orders of magnitude"?

The numbers are the fun part, and they are genuinely hard to hold in your head. Here are four attempts to make them physical.

Lift a sack of sugar, or detonate 27 megatons?

Take an Arecibo-to-Arecibo radio link over ten thousand light years, at a delivery speed of c/1000, with no shielding costs charged to the matter. The efficiency gain of inscribed matter (at ρ = 1023 bits/kg) over radiation is about 5.7 × 1016.

So if the energy to deliver the matter were equivalent to raising a two-kilogram bag of sugar one metre off the ground, the energy needed to radiate the same message would be a 27 megaton nuclear blast.

You'd need a galaxy ten times bigger than the visible universe before radiation wins

Radiation looks like it should have an advantage: it covers more targets at once. But to be even remotely competitive, the radiation must be collimated — and the tighter the collimation, the fewer star systems get illuminated. That requirement vitiates the broadcast advantage entirely.

Put it the other way round: send isotropically into a spherical galaxy of uniform stellar density, and charge matter the full cost of a shielded package to every single star in the volume. The breakeven galaxy — where there are finally enough stars that radiation becomes more efficient — is about ten times larger than the visible universe.

How long is long?

Because of shielding, the argument favours long messages over short "we exist" pings. Stefan Lovgren of National Geographic sensibly asked exactly how long "long" is.

For the Arecibo example at ten thousand light years, inscribed matter beats radiation for messages larger than about 1014 bits — roughly the written information in the Library of Congress.

How well can we aim?

For grossly macroscopic particles, Heisenberg is irrelevant. What matters is random gravitational perturbation en route, from the pull of stars whose placement we don't precisely know. It turns out aiming is relatively unimportant.

Mass information density

Bits per kilogram, from Voyager to lithium

The whole argument turns on ρ, how many bits you can pack into a kilogram. The span from a gold phonograph record to a protein is nineteen orders of magnitude — which is why the conclusion is so lopsided.

Musings

Where the idea leads if you let it

Cosmically selfish genes

If civilizations at any given stellar location are short-lived — through celestial or geological calamity, or self-inflicted — there could be a survival benefit to seeding the cosmos with compelling records of a civilization, in the hope of eventual adoption by fledgling civilizations or absorption by older ones.

But under the assumption of impermanence, literal biological seeding of likely habitats — Crick's panspermia — seems much more likely, because it confers a selective advantage of sorts. Civilizations that seed may survive in some form; those that don't may perish completely.

I have a soft spot for comets as delivery vehicles. They release material during transit through the inner solar system and they are highly visible. The annoying fact is that their orbits are stable only for tens of thousands of years rather than hundreds of millions, which doesn't leave local denizens much time to acquire the technology to reach out and grab one. For biological seeding, though, comets look decent. More on this.

Is life the message?

Michael Crichton made exactly this point — that life might be the message, and explicitly that it is more efficient to send a package than to radiate — in his 1969 novel The Andromeda Strain, pp. 222–225. Courtesy of Rich Howard.

Of motes and matter

One admittedly crazy image I cannot shake: arrays of computer chips using MEMS catapults to move information instead of optical or electromagnetic bus lines. Eventually I nagged myself into doing the calculation. Wafer-to-wafer communication over 10 cm at about 0.3 m/s, against infrared laser communication with 5 µm apertures.

The matter efficiency comes out at Ω = 5.2 × 10−14ρ. So tossing little magnetic chits back and forth would be roughly 5000 times more energy-efficient than laser communication — and if an STM tip inscribed the surface of an SiO2 chit, more than a thousand times better still. Smart dust tossing inscribed dust may not be so crazy after all.

There's one born every minute

New Scientist once profiled a now-defunct service — racket — whereby you called a 900 number to leave voice messages for ET, to be beamed into space with a satellite dish. They contacted me, and since silence can imply complicity, I did the calculation. I suspect customers had little hope of getting their messages across. But people bought pet rocks in the '70s, so perhaps there were plenty of takers.

It's always nice when your work gets called seminal, whether it actually is or not. In truth, without quantifying the reception problem and figuring out where to look without spending billions, a search for inscribed matter will be a tough sell both inside and outside the SETI community — even if we never hear distant intelligent radio signals, at least SETI telescopes do useful astronomy as a sideline. My guess is that inscribed matter, if any is ever found, will turn up en passant during solar system exploration rather than through a dedicated search.
