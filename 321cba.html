Einstein V. Roberts


The Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court, Esq. John Roberts, recently asked, ‘‘What unique perspective does a minority student bring to a physics class?’’ This seemingly reasonable but fundamentally flawed question stirred in me a powerful and multifaceted resonance/dissonance.   Further, it compels a response in this my sixty-fourth year of life as an American, as an African-American theoretical physicist and, interestingly, as an amateur Einstein historian.

Some personal history seems important.  I fell in love at the age of 17 with theoretical physics.  I was admitted to M.I.T.  in 1969 during the earliest days of affirmative action.  However, having attended high school in central Florida, my preparation was certainly uneven and the early going was rough.  Nonetheless, along with other minority classmates I persevered,  fell more deeply in love with each passing year, and eventually graduated with degrees in physics and mathematics.   I am currently a chaired Professor of Physics at the University of Maryland, a member of various national academies and President Obama's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, and I frequently appear on the PBS science series, NOVA.

However, if not for Affirmative Action, I would likely have not then (nor now) been admitted to MIT.  In fact, quoting M.I.T. ex-president Paul Gray, "PUT HIS QUOTE IN HERE."   Like the journeys of many of my minority colleagues,  America's original sin casts a long shadow that persists even without the de jure racism of the pre-Civil Rights era.  But more importantly, that shadow is being cast on our children who still do not enjoy the same levels of wealth, preparation and presumed intellectual capacity as their White counterparts.

For instance, two other justices on the Supreme Court made contemporaneous comments which reveal the persistence of traditional tropes about minority students and affirmative action in colleges and universities. While apparently Justices Thomas and Scalia have faith in some godlike ability to accurately,  and with perfect fidelity, measure a priori what constitutes merit in students from diverse demographic groups, no scientist I have ever encountered would endorse such a viewpoint. 

Furthermore, study after study reveals that diverse groups are better at solving problems.  So, what does a minority student bring to a physics class? The question ignores the fact that homogeneity exacts a toll.   Nonetheless,  the answer is that minorities bring the same things that minorities have brought to every American endeavor -- a different perspective, creativity, jazz!

From my perspective as an African American, and like Justice Thomas, a direct beneficiary of Affirmative Action, the real question before the Supreme Court of the United States is whether it is prepared to obliterate a tool that has demonstrably moved us a bit out of the nightmare shadow which Albert Einstein described as a ‘‘disease of white people.’’

Time and Providence will reveal soon enough all opinions to be rendered in the case of Einstein v. Roberts within the context of physics, and the case of Fisher v. University of Texas in the Supreme Court. However, it will be left to future generations to render judgment on our efforts in the now centuries long battle for moral uplift against the shadow of structural racism despite its de jure big brother having been (seemingly) vanquished.


S. James Gates
John H. Toll Professor of Physics
