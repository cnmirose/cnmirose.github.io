To recap, the result which spurred all this flap is based  on a theoretical answer to the following simple question:

(Q) What is the most energy efficient way to send a message from point A to point B.

(A) If you restrict yourself to radiative methods (as opposed to guided wave like fiber) and don't worry about corruption in transit,  it turns out that bit for bit, writing the message down in some  medium and literally tossing it to the destination is usually most efficient and often by many orders of magnitude.  This was a  big surprise!  The results appear in a paper on my web page.

The next set of questions for which I'd seek funding involve the message integrity when there is  a chance of corruption (as in a long journey) and the energetics of  message inscription and readout.  The assumption is that you have a  dense storage medium -- RNA and DNA are (radiation-fragile) examples,  but STM methods give reasonable bit densities as well. So, the N word  (nano) popped up pretty quickly -- nanostorage and nanoassembly most  obviously  -- and therefore the following questions:

(Q) What are theoretical limits on how densely information can be coded as ordered mass?

Note: this is a different question than storage technologists usually consider.  As Shannon gave bounds which are independent of HOW you do  your communication, medium/method-independent bounds are what's needed  here.  There are also sticky issues of modeling/coping with  high energy particle insults that occur in space (or in terrestrial  systems exposed to radiation).

(Q) What are the energetics of inscription and readout?

The late Rolf Landauer has already answered this question from a  theoretical perspective, but the practicalities are interesting.

The answers will be interesting at a number of levels, perhaps even  some practical ones in the area of storage technology. Of course, the fundamental question of whether it's truly better to send  a written message through the vastness of interstellar space as opposed to a radiated one (which is what we seem to be looking for currently),  and what form such a message would need to take is the  one which captures my fancy.  And it is this result which could  ultimately have the most impact since it might help resolve the nagging old Fermi Paradox of "where are they?/are they out there?"  It's not often that basic research in communications can even attempt to lay claim to something this grand (smile).

So, I guess this letter is really addressed to everyone.  The basic question (at this point) is whether such work has a chance of finding a home at NSF, either in a particular area or perhaps shared by several areas (astrophysics, for instance).
