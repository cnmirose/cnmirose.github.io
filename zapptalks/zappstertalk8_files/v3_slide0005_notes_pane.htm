The quality is poor relative to the newer formats, like DVDs.  We believe that people will come to expect better quality because of DVDs and high definition programming, and demand it because of larger screen TVs.  The DSL will only deliver one program at a time, to households with multiple TVs.  DSL cannot be offered at 1 megabit rates to homes that are several miles from the central office. Moreover it is relatively expensive, currently costing as much as premium cable service, even without content charges.
And all of this discussion is just about the last leg of the trip-- the DSL loop or the cellular system.  Moving those bits through the network is also a big problem.  We think of the Internet as almost free, but that is based on flat rates for today’s volume.  If we want to deliver as little as one hour a day of DVD quality video to the average home, we are talking about moving a volume of information 1000 times as large as the Internet today. We cannot assume that such a huge volume can be delivered at the same low flat rates. Sophisticated caching and delivery strategies will be needed, at significant extra cost.
And of course this “solution” is just for the home.  It doesn’t solve the other half of the problem-- delivering these files to people on the move.  The bottom line is that transmission technology to people at home and on the move isn’t improving fast enough to meet the challenge of giant files-- in fact the technology that we use is not growing in speed as fast as the file is growing in size.  By compressing the video and using the fastest lines we have, we can barely get by today, while the files keep growing in size and quantity.
People think that we are gaining ground on the problem, because they don’t see how the content is growing.  The truth is we’re like a surfer who’s moving slower than the wave-- in a few years the mismatch between file sizes and transmission capacity to the home will have grown larger, not smaller.
We need a technology that is evolving as fast as the content, and we actually have one.  Strangely, it’s a technology that we hardly notice because it has become so ubiquitous ...
