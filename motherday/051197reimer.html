Mom doesn't want much -- she wants everything

DR. CHARLES FLATTER, a child-development expert at the University of Maryland, writes in the May issue of Sesame Street Parents that dads and kids should not plan an "all-day, pro-Mom blowout" for today, Mother's Day.

Instead, he suggests a brief, modest celebration with gifts limited to hand-painted pictures or hand-made cards. Anything more, he says to parents of younger children, creates a level of emotion that kids can't handle.

Less is better, says Flatter from College Park, where he is a professor of human development and assistant director of the Institute for Child Study.

"Kids don't handle their own birthday parties very well. Their anticipation is so high and their ability to handle space and time is so minimal. And it can be confusing for them. Mother already had a birthday; why does she get two days?"

Two? I'm thinking 365.

Short? Modest? No expensive gifts?

Aren't we glad Flatter isn't our party planner!

I hate to disagree with an expert, and I don't want to scar the kids, but I'm looking for a ticker-tape parade down Fifth Avenue in New York today. I expect to spend Mother's Day waving to an adoring public from the back of an open convertible.

After 15 years in the motherhood game, I've moved way beyond burnt toast and juice on a tray in bed. Hand-made cards and flowers picked from my own garden don't do it for me anymore. I used to request tomato plants for Mother's Day, and an hour to myself to plant them.

No more.

I think the day ought to start with a church service consecrating my sainted, martyred self.

From there, it is on to a champagne brunch to fortify me for a day during which I will not lift a finger, to be concluded at a lavish dinner I didn't cook.

My husband will be so busy paying attention to me, he will not be able to read the Sunday paper, and my kids will have to wear outfits like those worn by children in catalogs, speak in whispers and use all the manners I have taught but never seen evidence of.

I'm sure Flatter's suggestions make good sense to fellow child development specialists, not to mention husbands and children, but they do not sit very well with those of us who are on the giving end of adoration for the rest of the year and expect to make up for it on Mother's Day.

Flatter, who is a regular columnist with Sesame Street Parents and is a member of their board of advisers, tells fathers that children love to have their ideas acknowledged, so children should take the lead in planning for Mother's Day.

It's my day, and I disagree.

Ask any kid his opinion, and you will hear more than you want to hear, and if I know mine, they will suggest that mother spend Mother's Day on the other side of the planet so they can do what they want.

Flatter also suggests that gift-giving be minimized, that little construction paper offerings are fine, that expensive gifts aren't the point.

Well then, what is the point? What is homage without gift-giving? What is worship without sacrifice? Mother's Day gifts need to be expensive, and there need to be lots of them, and any suggestion to the contrary is obviously being made by the man who has to take the kids shopping for those gifts.

"A great deal depends on how the spouse feels about Mother's Day," said Flatter, making my point. "Some fathers are not intrigued by the idea of celebrating Mother's Day because of their relationships with their own mothers."

Hey. Not my problem. I'm the mother this day is about.

Flatter's concern is for the emotional well-being of the young child, and that is admirable but it is misplaced.

On Mother's Day, the only emotions to be concerned about are the mother's emotions. 'Cause if she ain't happy, ain't nobody happy.
