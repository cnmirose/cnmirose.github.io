CONFIDENTIAL
 

 

ZAPPSTER
 
A Network for the Delivery of Large-File Information
 
 
 
1.  Executive Summary

A commuter passing through the railroad terminal downloads four hours of entertainment to a small terminal in her pocket.  On her way home, she watches the news on her laptop, and the first part of a movie.  Later, at home, she plugs the terminal into her entertainment center, finishes the movie and watches a network program she missed the week before, then relaxes with the latest CD released by her favorite entertainer.  Going to work the next day, she begins to listen to a book in audio form.  All this and much more is possible with a unique new delivery network called ZAPPSTER.

 

ZAPPSTER is a unique information system—a network of kiosks, designed to deliver a rich menu of very large files to small, portable terminals, at very low cost.  The files can include movies, network television programming, music, books in audio and text formats, magazines and newspapers, local geographic information, computer software and personal information.  The terminals will both store and transport this information, and will have the ability to display it on a variety of portable and home (or office) based devices (e.g., TVs, PCs, music players, digital VCRs, laptops and palmtops).  ZAPPSTER therefore combines the features of a video store, music store, bookstore, computer software outlet, news stand, message service and local information service.  It offers an exciting new way to distribute entertainment for use in the home, and creates a new market for mobile entertainment and information services. 

 

The delivery of a “personal” selection of very large information files (such as music and movies) to the home has proved impractical to date, and doing so for people on the move is far beyond the capabilities of current and planned wireless systems. The fastest wireline connections to the home are still inadequate or too expensive for video, and the Internet itself is dwarfed by the potential demand. If only one hour of DVD-quality video programming per day were to be selected on demand and delivered to the home, the quantity of information would be roughly 1000 times the traffic carried on the Internet today. Thus, while broadcast, cable and satellite systems offer a wide range of mass-media entertainment to the home at attractive rates, the vision of choosing any entertainment we want remains stubbornly over the horizon.

 

In the wireless world, the situation is even more difficult.  At current cellular bit rates and costs, the download of a DVD-quality movie would take several hundred hours and cost several thousand dollars. “Third generation” cellular systems will be fast enough to enable today’s Internet services for people on the move, but a movie download would still take 5-50 hours.  Worse yet, the cost of delivering Information through these systems will see only modest reductions.  This will discourage even the types of usage we see on the Internet today, and prohibit the delivery of very large files.

Clearly, a breakthrough is needed.  ZAPPSTER provides this breakthrough through a unique combination of storage and download capabilities, supported by a novel distribution network. 

 

2.  ZAPPSTER Overview

There are 4 major elements to the ZAPPSTER system—kiosks, terminals, service centers, and a hybrid distribution network.

The visible element of ZAPPSTER is its network of kiosks.   These public access points will store a large and relatively stable menu of “popular” information, as well as special orders for individual customers. They will download this information at very high speeds to small, portable terminals.  Several means of download will provide flexible options for different situations, features and price points.

The terminals will download the information from the kiosks, transport it to the point of use, and support playback over a variety of portable and home-based TVs, computers, and audio players.   Some terminals will provide an integrated playback capability as well.

The entire library of ZAPPSTER content will be managed by regional service centers.  These highly automated centers will store a very large menu of content, and have access to additional content from a variety of sources.  They will supply both the popular menu and the special orders to the kiosks to meet customer needs at minimal cost. 

The service centers will distribute information to the kiosks over a unique hybrid network, utilizing both wireline and physical transportation modes.  The wireline mode will allow the timely delivery of time-sensitive information, while the physical transport will allow huge volumes of content to be moved at very low cost.

As discussed above, ZAPPSTER can provide an unusually broad array of entertainment and information content, including DVD movies, television programs, music CDs, audio books, newspapers, magazines, email, voicemail and even commercial software packages.  The combination of a popular menu and special orders makes the total menu virtually infinite, and the combination of these different forms of content creates a uniquely rich “retail” environment.   Moreover, ZAPPSTER can create service and revenue options that do not exist today.   For example, ZAPPSTER provides a channel for the distribution of “old” television programs that the customer has missed or wishes to see again.  Current programming can be downloaded and watched at a time and place that is convenient for the viewer (even before the scheduled network time) and without setting up a VCR.  Content doesn’t have to be returned in a short time-period, as with movie rentals.  The number of viewings can be limited if desired.  Different charging options such as flat rate for old content and pay-per-view for new content can be accommodated.  Good security can be provided to prevent redistribution of content (unlike DVD).  Advertising can be inserted for a particular customer or customer profile, and the customer can even provide feedback to the advertiser or content producer (during the next visit).  New formats, such as high-definition video, can easily be accommodated. Maps, directories, advertising, and other local information can be provided for the geographic area near the kiosk (and for any other geographic area, by special order). Software, and software updates, can be distributed.

 

Moreover, since the terminal has a very large capacity and the capability to interface with laptops and PC’s, it may also serve as a repository of “personal” files, and may even be “synchronized” with other storage devices via the ZAPPSTER kiosk.  This could create a new paradigm in personal computing, in which the user carries that part of his information that is personal in a very small package, and can interface that information with any available computing device.

 

Thus, while the initial service descriptions and economics of ZAPPSTER presented here are heavily weighted toward familiar entertainment applications, this system has the potential to spawn a wide range of new services and behaviors.

The four elements of the ZAPPSTER system will now be discussed in greater detail.

 

2.1   Kiosk

The ZAPPSTER kiosk will be a dramatically styled, highly visible installation, somewhat like an ATM for entertainment and information.  It will be installed at convenient locations, such as retail stores, gas stations and public transportation terminals.  These locations are relatively secure from both tampering and weather, simplifying the design and cost of the kiosks. The footprint will be small, thus reducing the cost of space.  In fact, since ZAPPSTER will attract customer traffic, retailers may find the kiosk a highly desired “tenant.” 

 

 

The ZAPPSTER kiosk will have the capacity to store a large and varied ”popular menu”.  Based on current magnetic disk storage technology, a compact and low-cost storage shelf will hold hundreds of DVD-quality movies, thousands of music CDs and books (in text and audio form), and a variety of other information (e.g., network television programs, newspapers, magazines, email, voicemail and advertising).  The storage capacity is also sufficient to provide additional descriptive information for this content, such as book reviews, samples of music and preview clips for movies.  In only a few years, the same shelf will have 10 times this capacity and cost less than $100.  This will allow the kiosk to offer a selection comparable to the combined inventory of a large video store, music store and bookstore.

 

If necessary, the kiosk will be capable of operating as a stand-alone server, with no wireline connections.  The preferred configuration, however, will have some form of wireline connection to the service centers.  This may take the form of a low-speed telephone line or cellular connection, or (preferably) a higher speed cable or DSL connection.  A low-speed connection would be used for security, billing and placing special orders.  A higher speed connection would allow certain forms of time-critical information to be downloaded to the kiosk, including news, financial information and personal information such as email and voicemail.  As an added service, a kiosk with a fast wireline connection could offer real-time Internet access to its customers.

 

The kiosk will provide additional disk space and a high-speed input port for uploading additional information.  Service personnel will visit the kiosk on a daily schedule, with a portable storage device similar to the storage shelf of the kiosk.  Through a plug connection, the service personnel will upload new files, including additions to the popular menu at all kiosks, and special orders or personal information to be picked up at individual kiosks.

 

The kiosk will allow several methods of download, each with it’s own costs and performance features.  The fastest and least expensive will be a plug-in socket for the terminal.  This will allow the smallest, lowest cost terminal, and will download a movie in a few seconds.  In cases where the identities of the desired files have already been stored in the terminal, or where an order has been placed at the service center, this will allow a very fast transaction, which is desirable for high traffic locations such as commuter terminals.  A passing commuter will insert his terminal into the socket, and in a few seconds the download will be complete. In cases where the user wishes to “browse” the menu or preview selections, the expanded transaction time would require many plug-in positions.  Moreover, for very simple terminals that have no display capabilities, each plug-in position will need a display.  To encourage fast transactions and minimize the number of plug-in positions at busy locations, some “express” sockets will not provide displays and will only serve terminals with preset orders.

 

A second download option, broadband radio, will be somewhat slower and will require terminals that are equipped with batteries and displays, increasing both their size and cost.  This technique has the advantage that it will allow the download to take place from distances of 10-30 yards, and will accommodate multiple users browsing the menu and previewing selections.  Radio access will also allow download from a retail storefront when the store is closed, and will allow download to someone in a nearby automobile, providing increased convenience and security.  The download rate will depend on available bandwidth and complexity, but rates of several hundred megabits to more than a Gigabit per second are realistic.  At these rates, download of a DVD-quality movie will require 30-90 seconds.  A narrowband uplink channel will provide communications from the terminal to the kiosk.

 

An infrared interface would provide an intermediate range solution, with some of the advantages of radio.

 

In addition to these proprietary ZAPPSTER interfaces, the kiosk can provide standardized wireless interfaces, such as Bluetooth and 802.11.  These will allow secondary services, such as web access and email, to be provided to suitably equipped terminals.  While not fast enough to download very large files, such interfaces could be used to download smaller ZAPPSTER files, such as books (in text form) or newspapers.

 

We envision that the installed cost of the kiosk will be less than $10,000.  This low cost, plus the nature of the installations, makes physical security a tractable problem.  On the other hand, the content will have great value, and must be stored in a secure, scrambled form with a key that is inaccessible to the public.  A second form of encryption will be applied for download to the customer, and for customer identification.  Billing will be by prepaid or credit card accounts.

 

2.2   Portable Terminals

A variety of customer terminals are possible, with different cost, size and service characteristics.  The “plug-in only” terminal will be a very small, low-cost storage device with the ability to decrypt content and play it back through other devices.  Its power can be provided by the devices with which it interfaces, so that no battery is required.  By adding a simple display to this terminal, it will be possible to view a stored menu and enter order instructions, making the process more user-friendly and efficient. 

 

A high-end terminal will provide both the plug interface and the high-speed radio interface described above, with its own battery and display.  This will allow an efficient multi-user browsing experience, including the download of menus, previews and critical reviews.  Also, as described above, it will allow the interaction to take place from outside a closed business, or from the comfort and security of a parked car.  Download of the ordered content will generally utilize the radio as well, but in certain cases (e.g., a high-traffic location such as a commuter terminal) the user may wish (or be required) to use the plug-in connection for a faster download. 

 

As discussed above, an alternative infra-red interface would provide an additional combination of performance characteristics and cost. 

 

Additional types of terminal, such as laptops and palmtops, with standard plug or radio interfaces, may also be afforded limited Internet access or ZAPPSTER service through the kiosk.

 

We envision the terminal with 100 Gigabytes of storage, radio download and display capability will cost about $300 in 2005, and that a terminal with 20 Gigabytes of storage, no radio and no display would cost as little as $50 in 2003.

 

2.3   Service Centers

The regional service centers are the repository for all the information files available for distribution, and can gather additional files from a variety of sources.  They will receive orders from customers, schedule files for distribution to the kiosks, and control the download of these files into the hybrid network which delivers them to the kiosks (see 2.4, below).   Because of the low cost of data storage, there will be an inherent bias toward storing all available files at each regional center, rather than downloading them from a national center on demand.  For this reason, it is unlikely that a national service center will be required.

 

The key element of the service center is its scheduling software.  This proprietary software accepts special orders, adds them to other information that must be distributed, takes into account the capabilities of the hybrid network, schedules deliveries and confirms orders to the customers.

 

The highly automated service centers will require little staffing.  Order taking will be automated, using the ZAPPSTER website, Touchtone signaling over the telephone network, and orders submitted through the kiosks.  The website will be housed at the service center, and will provide email and voicemail service for customers who wish this information to be distributed to particular kiosks.  Other daily information such as news and stock market quotes will arrive at the service center periodically.  Customer profiles and account information will also be stored at the service center.

 

 

4.1.4  Hybrid Network

As discussed, a central problem of implementing any network for the delivery of entertainment is the vast size of the information files.  We envision an information volume that could dwarf the Internet, yet we seek a very low cost solution.  The answer is found in the unique ZAPPSTER hybrid network, a combination or low or moderate rate wireline connections and storage devices which can be used to physically transport very large files at very low cost, on a predictable daily schedule.  This combination of wireline and physical transport, coupled with the scheduling software at the service center, allows ZAPPSTER to meet a variety of objectives.  Small personal files and news updates can be delivered as needed, on short notice.  Menu updates and special orders can be delivered on a predictable schedule, so that customers can be assured of delivery at a known time.

 

Specifically, we envision that the service center software will control the download of files from the central cache to either the wireline links or (most often) to an array of portable caches.  These portable caches will then be physically transported to the kiosks, by service personnel, on a regular scheduled route.  At each location, the cache will be connected to the kiosk and the new menu items and special orders will be uploaded.  We expect that the upload process will take several minutes, and that a single delivery person will be able to service about 10 kiosks, in an area of up to 100 square miles, during a 2-3 hour period.  This will allow orders placed by early afternoon to be available for pickup during the evening commute.  Because a large fraction of the delivery period is spent moving between kiosks, and because we desire to minimize the total delivery period, it may be preferable to use a commercial delivery service such as UPS for this function.  The size of the service area covered by a service center will depend on the trade-off between caching costs and delivery time.  In very large areas, it may be preferable to create low-cost, unstaffed satellite service centers, with some, but not all of the content that is available at the main service center.  This implies a two-step distribution for some rarely requested content, but would shorten the delivery time for most special orders.

 

This hybrid network and scheduling software can distribute the vast quantity of information required, at low cost, while meeting attractive delay constraints.  In addition to the large popular menu of content that will always be available at the kiosks, special orders that are placed by noon will be available during the evening commute, and time-sensitive information will be updated as needed, using the wireline capability.

 

4.2  Cost and Capacity for an Example Configuration

Based on available data for movie rentals alone, we estimate that about 3% of the population will rent a movie during a typical day.  For this exercise, we will assume that 15% of this existing demand will move to the ZAPPSTER network, and that an equal amount of new usage (commuters and travelers) will be generated.  For a population of 10,000 in the kiosk area, this implies 100 downloads per day, and a total download time of 30 minutes per day.  Assuming the kiosk has an installed cost of $10,000 and a monthly revenue requirement of $400, this implies a kiosk cost of 13 cents per download. 

 

A dedicated delivery person working 4 hours each day to serve 10 kiosks will add another 10 cents per download.  A satellite cache might cost about $50,000 (installed), and serve 10 local delivery areas (100 kiosks), adding another 2 cents per download.  A regional center serving 1000 kiosks, costing $500,000, and employing 10 people, would add an additional 2 cents per download. 

 

Even these conservative numbers suggest a total distribution cost of less than 30 cents per movie.  Moreover, the costs identified are essentially independent of the volume delivered.  Even with only one download at a time, the kiosk could easily serve 5-10 times the estimated traffic, and a terminal in an airport or commuter terminal could download thousands of files per day.  The service center, caches and delivery personnel also have the capacity to deliver an almost open-ended quantity without increasing the level of investment or expense.  The more we deliver, the lower the cost per delivery will be.  In this regard, it should be noted that the above example is based on movies only, and does not consider the other forms of entertainment and information that would be distributed as well.  We believe that a distribution cost of less than 10 cents for a file the size of a movie is realizable.

 

It would be logical to first deploy this service at major urban commuter bus and rail terminals.  Such locations should generate heavy traffic, since a customer with a terminal may want to download something every day.  It would be logical to place the service center nearby, and to equip several kiosks to efficiently capture the flow of commuters.  Such a configuration with only a few thousand regular customers will achieve the same low cost per download as can be achieved in full deployment, allowing gradual, cost-effective growth. 

 

We expect that the early customers for local kiosks with be young people, who are “early adapters” and major users of video rental, technophiles, and heavy users of video rentals and music CDs.

 

2.4  Competitive position

The initial positioning of the ZAPPSTER service will provide a competitive alternative to existing video rental stores.  Using a low cost terminal, the user will download movies for only pennies more than the cost of the content.  The investment represented by the kiosk, service center and hybrid distribution network will be far less than that of the alternative video rental stores serving the same region, and far fewer employees will be needed.  No physical inventory is required, and selections will never be out of stock.  Music, books, and network programming can be offered, as well as local and personal information.  No returns are needed, and security can be provided for recent releases.  Advertising can be targeted.  The low capital investment will allow more locations to be provided, making the kiosks more generally available than video stores.  Their small footprint will allow them to be installed in high-traffic areas, where a video store would not be possible.  Stand-alone kiosks can offer a large popular menu without daily deliveries of special orders, and can serve low-density areas where a video store would be impractical.  Overall, the competitive position of ZAPPSTER relative to the video rental store seems attractive.

 

In addition to tapping the existing market for video entertainment, ZAPPSTER will simultaneously serve the other markets discussed above, while creating a new market in mobile entertainment.  Commuters and travelers will have access to the same wide variety of content and low prices.  Laptops will become portable video centers, and a market in portable TVs and music players will emerge.

 

In the long term, it is possible that broadband access to the home will someday deliver a similarly large menu of content on demand, and provide a competitive alternative to the ZAPPSTER kiosk for the take-home market.   Real time viewing will require about 5 megabits/ second, however, or five times the current DSL speeds.  At current DSL speeds of 1 megabit per second, movies would have to be downloaded to a storage device, at about 10 hours per movie.  High definition programming will require even higher bit rates.  As discussed earlier, sending these levels of load through the network will add dramatically to the volume of traffic on the Internet.  All these factors suggest that such capabilities will not be available soon.  Thus, while we believe that our take-home entertainment market will eventually be impacted by broadband to the home, we believe that this risk is not imminent, and will be offset by the emerging market for mobile entertainment and new information services. 

 

As discussed above, the kiosk also offers some interesting opportunities for targeted advertising, since the system operator will have the ability to develop “profiles” of individual customers.  For example, the service provider can eliminate previews of films that the user has already seen, and insert others based on the customer’s established preferences.  Local businesses might want to insert advertising targeted at specific profiles or even specific customers.  Networks may prefer to substitute such targeted information for their usual network advertising, when delivering content through a kiosk.  Such targeted advertising can represent a significant (and perhaps dominant) revenue stream for this business.  One can even imagine distributing a “free” terminal that would not allow the customer to fast-forward past the advertising.

 

4.4 Timeline

We see the ZAPPSTER as evolving through a three-phase process. 

 

Phase 1, the research phase, has been completed. 

 

In phase 2, prototype designs must be created, refined and shown to perform well in realistic scenarios. This will lead to detailed specifications for product development.  Simultaneously, in phase 2, it will be necessary to refine the business and system models, explore the necessary business relationships for service (particularly the relationships with content providers), and form the development team. 

 

In phase 3, software and hardware will be designed for manufacture, business relationships will be finalized, beta tests will be conducted and the first commercial service will be deployed.  Since the development of the high-speed radio link is probably the most time-consuming element of the deployment effort, it will be desirable to initiate service with the plug-in terminals described earlier.  On this basis we estimate that a three-year interval to first commercial deployment could be achieved, with availability of the radio terminals about 2 years later.
