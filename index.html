Christopher Rose

Wireless & beyond

Communication and information theory applied to just about everything — which, it turns out, is a defensible research program and not only a character flaw.

Interaction is usually defined by what we can observe about outcomes: reactants move toward equilibrium, momentum and energy are conserved, elements of an organism signal each other chemically, mechanically, electrically. Frame those observations mathematically and the equations confer predictive power — which is the whole point of science. However, any interaction is a communication, and communication implies information exchange. So to a communication theorist trained in the physical sciences, one question is inescapable:

What are all these interacting elements saying to one another, how are they saying it, and can I join the conversation?

Who

The short version, told slowly

Chris received the S.B. (1979), S.M. (1981) and Ph.D. (1985) degrees, all from MIT. Following graduate school he joined AT&T Bell Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, as a member of the Network Systems Research Department, where he regaled his peers with levitated-center-conductor high-Tc superconducting cables, annoyed them by showing that random lightwave network architectures perform about as well as carefully sculpted ones, and puzzled them with odd applications of cellular automata.

In 1990, when Arno Penzias told everyone in "Area 11" to go to academe if they weren't keenly interested in the corporate bottom line — including his Nobel partner Bob Wilson — Chris was congratulated on his timing, having joined Electrical & Computer Engineering at Rutgers the very next week.

He stayed at Rutgers until 2015, when he moved to Brown as Professor of Engineering and Associate Dean of the Faculty for Special Initiatives. In 2020 he was appointed Associate Provost for STEM Initiatives, and served as the inaugural Director of STEMJazz Programs until 2026. He is a Fellow of the IEEE, cited "for contributions to wireless communication systems theory," and served as a full member of the Army Science Board (2013–2014).

He was Associate Director of WINLAB (1999–2007). His current technical interests include applications of communication and information theory to just about everything: biology, nano- micro- and macro-mechanical systems, and — tantalizingly — maybe even supersymmetry, by way of Jim Gates' intriguing Adinkra formalism.

Past wireless interests include novel mobile communication networks, genetic algorithms applied to control problems in networks, and interference avoidance — universal radios negotiating peaceful coexistence in the wireless ecology of the 5 GHz U-NII bands. That work, with Sennur Ulukus and Roy Yates, received the 2003 IEEE Marconi Prize Paper Award in Wireless Communications. Here's a picture of the ecstatic authors at the Globecom 2004 ceremony (credit: Aylin Yener).

And for fun — as an outgrowth of research on opportunistic communications — he took on a problem everyone has wondered about at least once: how will first contact actually happen? The interesting twist is that it can be far more efficient for distant little green people to send information-bearing physical artifacts than electromagnetic signals, which is seemingly at odds with current SETI wisdom. That story is over here.

Research

Six threads, one question

Different systems, same move each time: treat the interaction as a channel, then ask what the channel can and cannot carry.


It from channel

Talking particles

If two billiard balls collide, how many bits did they exchange? Conservation laws turn out to be the null space of the interaction channel. This is the current obsession.

Molecular

Communicating with molecules

What are the fundamental limits when your symbols are actual molecules — released, diffusing, arriving late and out of order? Timing, payload, and 40 bps using alcohol and a photoionization detector.

SETI

Write, don't radiate

Sending inscribed matter beats radiating photons across interstellar distances, by a lot. Nature cover, September 2004 — plus an astounding amount of press, a NY Times editorial, and a cartoon competition.
