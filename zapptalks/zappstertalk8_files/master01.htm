For over eleven years, WINLAB has focused or wireless systems, particularly wireless information systems.   We have both teaching professors and engineers with decades of industry experience, and we are sponsored by more than 20 companies, so we try to see the whole picture—not just technology, but services, costs and quality issues as well. It’s only when those things come together that great systems are created.
Several years ago, we became convinced that the wireless systems of today, including the third generation systems being proposed for the coming decade, were not suitable for the very large file sizes that we want for information and entertainment.  This fact has been masked by talk of high data rates like 384 Kilobits per second, or even the megabits per second that such systems may offer under optimum conditions. Combined with compression techniques that can reduce music or movie to smaller files, they conjure up an image of delivering streaming audio and even video, to people on the move.  What they don’t say is that while these systems are marginally fast enough to deliver a reduced quality of video, the cost per delivered bit will remain stubbornly high.  People who were turned on by an Internet that is essentially free are going to be shocked by the toll charges on the wireless superhighway.    Video and music to our cellphones may be technologically feasible, but it is not economically viable.   Does this matter?  One could argue that email and some web-based services are all we need in our cell phones.  We think this flies in the face of what is happening out there.  We look out there and see content in ever richer forms.  Movies are going to DVD formats and will go beyond that to high definition.  Software packagers get bigger.  Games get more elaborate.  More music videos are produced.  More channels of TV programming are released.  Laptops and palms play movies and music.  And people on the move have special needs for information-- they need maps, images and detailed information about their surroundings.  Yet the inadequacies of wireless are leading to a world in which all that content is denied to people on the move, where they spend so much of their lives. 
We evolved a wireless system call Infostations to solve this problem.  Since cellular systems are used primarily for voice, they have to provide a low bit-rate signal in every cubic inch of the world.  A delivery system for giant files is just the opposite.  It has to provide very high bit rates, but it doesn’t need to cover every cubic inch of the world—just enough places to provide convenient access points as you move about. Infostations are base stations that cover maybe a few hundred feet.  They can provide a huge bit-rate in these “islands of coverage” – and do it at low cost.  They don’t have to be everywhere—just in enough places to give you the access you need as you move about.   With Infostations, we can reduce the cost of delivering a file by a factor of about 1000, while still providing frequent, convenient access.  Infostations can make all that large-file content available to people on the move. 
Then we looked at those Infostations scattered all around, and we realized that people on the move could access them today, without even using a radio, by simply plugging in their terminals.  The Infostation became the Zappster kiosk—sort of an ATM for information and entertainment where people could pause for a few seconds and “fill up.”  Instead of waiting for a few years while the radios got designed, the technology to make this happen exists today.   We can get started without radio, and we can use radio to make it better later on. And finally, we realized that many of the same problems we had been working on exist for people at home as well as people on the move.  There’s not enough low cost capacity to the home either.  Cable doesn’t give us free choice of what we get, and internet access, even with cable modems and DSL, is too slow for giant files. Nothing gives us unlimited access to see what we want, when we want it-- at an affordable price.  We released that we could create a new kind of system system that would do it all-- deliver giant files at low cost— to people at home and people on the move.  It could make that sea of content available to everyone-- what they want, when they want, and where they want-- and at a price they will be willing to pay.  And our system evolves smoothly-- from a simple but exciting entertainment service, to a global network for moving giant entertainment and information files—anywhere.   The technology that makes it possible will surprise you.
That’s the story we want to tell you today.
We need to start by asking what a giant file is, and why it’s hard to deliver.  One problem in answering that is that the answer keeps changing.  Not long ago, it was a PowerPoint presentation with ClipArt-- maybe 400 KiloBytes.  Then there were Music CD’s that contained 400 MegaBytes.  Now there are DVD movies that contain 4 GigaBytes. Most people don’t appreciate the huge difference in size between what we used to want to send, and what we now want to send.  One reason is that the terms change-- from Kilo to Mega to Giga, so the numbers don’t get bigger.  We went from 400 to 400 to 4 “something or others”.  Another reason is that the physical packages look the same-- the floppy disk that holds my PowerPoint, the music CD and the movie DVD look about the same size.  A third reason is that we now believe the Internet can do anything, so why worry about how big the file is.

You can see why cellular deliver of these files isn’t in the cards, but how about home delivery?
Let’s take another walk down memory lane.  In the 1980s, I bought a PC with a really fast modem-- about 10,000 bits per second.  Email seemed instantaneous, and I could send that PowerPoint file in 5 minutes-- kind of slow but it was acceptable. Today I can get DSL, of course, and get a million bits/second-- for $40/month.   I can download that Powerpoint file in 3 seconds-- but it would still take me over an hour to download that music CD or VCR quality movie, and 11 hours to download that DVD movie. The DVD is clearly not reasonable, but before we move on, let’s take a closer look at that VCR quality movie.  We seem to be able to deliver it in less time than it takes to watch it, so we could “stream” it to the home and watch it while it’s arriving.  This is a real possibility that people are experimenting with to deliver video on demand, so it deserves mention.  It’s a viable approach, but there are a number of problems ...
The quality is poor relative to the newer formats, like DVDs.  We believe that people will come to expect better quality because of DVDs and high definition programming, and demand it because of larger screen TVs.  The DSL will only deliver one program at a time, to households with multiple TVs.  DSL cannot be offered at 1 megabit rates to homes that are several miles from the central office. Moreover it is relatively expensive, currently costing as much as premium cable service, even without content charges. And all of this discussion is just about the last leg of the trip-- the DSL loop or the cellular system.  Moving those bits through the network is also a big problem.  We think of the Internet as almost free, but that is based on flat rates for today’s volume.  If we want to deliver as little as one hour a day of DVD quality video to the average home, we are talking about moving a volume of information 1000 times as large as the Internet today. We cannot assume that such a huge volume can be delivered at the same low flat rates. Sophisticated caching and delivery strategies will be needed, at significant extra cost. And of course this “solution” is just for the home.  It doesn’t solve the other half of the problem-- delivering these files to people on the move.  The bottom line is that transmission technology to people at home and on the move isn’t improving fast enough to meet the challenge of giant files-- in fact the technology that we use is not growing in speed as fast as the file is growing in size.  By compressing the video and using the fastest lines we have, we can barely get by today, while the files keep growing in size and quantity.  People think that we are gaining ground on the problem, because they don’t see how the content is growing.  The truth is we’re like a surfer who’s moving slower than the wave-- in a few years the mismatch between file sizes and transmission capacity to the home will have grown larger, not smaller. We need a technology that is evolving as fast as the content, and we actually have one.  Strangely, it’s a technology that we hardly notice because it has become so ubiquitous ...
That technology is the magnetic disk drive, like this.  In that 1980s computer I described, I had a 2 MegaByte disk.  Today, they come with 20-60 GigaBytes-- an increase of 10,000 times while those transmission rates were increasing by only 100 times.  Their capacity has doubled every year, and they’ve gotten cheaper as well.
When you’re designing systems for a mass market, you know you’re on the right track when your technology is sold by companies called “Dirt Cheap Drives”, who advertise “Blow Out Sales”.  We can buy a 3-1/2 inch drive like this that will hold 5 DVDs for about $100 bucks, or one that will hold 2 DVDs for $70. And they’ve gotten smaller as well. For PCs there was little need for hard disks to get smaller, but laptops required a smaller configuration, like this 2-1/2 inch 6 Gigabyte drive.  It can hold 1 1/2 DVDs, and in two years that will become 6 DVDs.  They’re even coming out as  PCMCIA cards with on board disks.  This one inch disk actually holds 1 Gigabyte --  a couple of music CDs or VCR quality movies .  In a couple of years, this will hold a DVD movie.  The capacity, cost and size performance of these drives is incredible. Hard drives are also fast.  We will soon be able to write that DVD movie onto a hard disk in 15 seconds, or a VCR quality movie in 2 seconds.  In a world where we are used to impressive learning curves, the magnetic hard disk stands out.  It’s capacity doubles every year, and will can write to it twice as fast every two years, while its cost and size keep dropping.  This is the technology we want to ride. You’re probably thinking “we were talking about delivering these files– what does storing all those bits have to do with delivering them?”
Ah, but that’s the Zappster magic-- we can turn storage into transmission. 
(THROW)
I just demonstrated a channel carrying 50 billion bits per second.
Now you’re probably thing “cute, but moving them ten feet doesn’t cut it.”  Yet you create such a channel several miles long every time you bring home a movie from the video store.  If it takes you ten minutes to drive home a DVD, you have moved the equivalent of 50 million bits per second– 50 times as much as DSL could have moved in the same time. In Zappster, we have used these incredible storage devices both to store and to move the bits, and as a result, we can move files so large they are impractical to move in any other way-- and very low cost.  I’m going to construct Zappster for you in stages– first as a simple alternative  to the video store, then as an expansion of the video store, then as a unique global network with incredible capacity.
We call our simplest service “Blockbuster in a Box”.  What that means is that we can store the equivalent of a Blockbuster store in something the size of an ATM.  Within two years, a multi-disk pack costing $5000 will store 2000 DVDs or 16,000 “VCR” quality movies.  You will plug in a Personal Data Pack-- we call it a PDP--  like this compact storage device, download a movie in a few seconds), take it home and watch it on your home TV. 
We see immediately that the PDP itself represents a new consumer product, but consider the rest of the comparison.  We have replaced a large store with a kiosk the size of an ATM, and costing perhaps $10,000.  It has no employees and no inventory– and nothing is ever out of stock.  That issue alone is a tremendous benefit– imagine knowing the movie you want will always be there. The kiosk can be located in a retail store, a mall, or a bus terminal, on a footprint of a few square feet.  It will draw traffic, so it will be considered an attractive addition to any retail environment.  Because it costs so little, it can be made more frequently and conveniently available to the public.
But these advantages are just the beginning!
The fact that we are downloading a digital file to a known customer has some very significant advantages.  Unlike DVDs, we can encrypt each download individually, and provide a key to the PDP that can’t be shared.  We can monitor and control the number of viewings, so we can even allow you to bring home several movies, and give you credit for those you don’t watch. There is nothing to return, so you save the second trip, and don’t have to watch it the same day. And because the download is “personal”, we can provide you with personalized advertising.  This might mean that you can order preview clips of movies that interest you, or that we could provide such clips based on your “profile”, eliminating those you have seen and providing new ones that are likely to be of interest based on your viewing history.  As you are probably thinking, this also raises the possibility of personalized advertising that goes beyond film clips– but more on that later.
It’s time to expand our horizon– in two dimensions.
First, there’s the WHERE dimension.  Today, most movies are watched in the home, but we see a big new market for viewing on the move– in planes and trains, busses and vans.  We already see kids watching tapes in the family van, and your laptop will play DVDs if you want it to– so we will want access to this content wherever we go, and we certainly don’t want to return it to the same place.  Zappster fits this need perfectly, and it can deliver formats for any purpose.  It can supply bits to a laptop or an NTSB signal to a home TV.  The file can be “VCR” quality for a small screen, or a high-definition image for home theater.
And of course, movies aren’t the only entertainment that can be downloaded in digital form.  The kiosk can offer a selection of CDs, and music in MP3 format for portable players.  Audio books are also large files that are becoming increasingly popular, and of course print formats would be trivial to handle as well. The Zappster kiosk can also deliver games. We believe that network TV programming represents a big opportunity, and that Zappster can be a whole new distribution channel for network programming. You could download the evening’s entertainment at the train terminal, start to watch it on the train, and then finish at home.  In addition to current programs, you could download programs you’ve missed, and programs you saw a long time ago, and want to see again.  For the networks, this is an opportunity to pick up viewers they missed, and as an added incentive, it can be delivered with more valuable, targeted advertising. 
Our content can go beyond entertainment as well, to news programming– not only TV and radio news, but newspapers and magazines.  Watch the news on the way home.  Get your local paper while traveling. 
Microsoft would find our kiosk an excellent way to sell their software.
Finally, there’s a whole new area of information that truly conjures up the mobile internet– local information and advertising.  Detailed maps and directories, images of the local environment, advertising for stores and restaurants– even local history and walking tours, can be provided in the large files we can easily download.  The message here is that Zappster can begin as a competitor for conventional video rental, but it has the potential to be much more than that.  It allows any form of digital information to be made available at a very low delivery cost.
By now you may have noticed that we have gone from some movies to a virtually open-ended menu----- and you may be wondering how we plan to put all that in a kiosk– even one that doubles its capacity every year.  The answer is that we can put a truly impressive amount into the kiosk– to be available to anyone passing by– and we can get the rest there on demand, using what we call our “hybrid network.”
Our plan is to store a much larger menu of content at a central location-- the Zappster Service Center.  Customers will order this content at our website or by phone, or at the kiosk, and it will be available for pickup at a selected kiosk a few hours later.  But delivering more content to the kiosk involves the same problems we have discussed for delivering it to the home, multiplied by the many orders we will have to fill-- and we can’t expect high speed wireline connection to be available at a typical kiosk location.  We needed a new solution.
The answer was once again to recognize that original lesson.  Our customer carrying the loaded PDP home represents a massive delivery rate-- and we can use that same technique of physical delivery to bring much greater amounts of content from the service center to the kiosk.  Instead of a PDP, we load the content into a cluster of disks similar to what we have described for the kiosk, and physically transport that device to the kiosks on a scheduled daily route.  At each kiosk, the delivery person will use a high speed connection to upload the new content into the kiosk.  The capacity of this physical delivery channel is so large that it is virtually infinite when compared to Internet connections, and the cost is essentially independent of the amount delivered.  In addition to the special orders, this physical delivery network will handle updates of the kiosk menu, including new movies and network programs.  Special orders placed by early afternoon will be available for pickup by the evening commute. We can use our own personnel to deliver the content, or contract with a commercial service like UPS, which would probably be cheaper and may be faster as well.
In addition to this physical delivery channel, we envision that there will be a DSL-type Internet connection to most kiosks.  This will allow last-minute updates to information like news and financial reports, and can be used where special orders do not justify a physical visit.  One additional possibility is that personal information such as large email files and voicemail could be delivered to kiosks for pickup, or that customers could use the kiosk as a public access portal. The entire process would be automated.  Customers would place orders– over the Internet for example, and the time of delivery would be automatically confirmed.  The content would be downloaded at the service center to the delivery disks and wireline connections automatically.
And finally, we come to the last step in our evolution– Zappster as a global network.  We have noted that we can’t economically deliver GigaBytes to the places people want them over landlines, but we know that where the broadband fiber network exists it had incredible capacity, and low cost.  By placing our service centers on this network, we can move our bits anywhere in the world for pennies a Gigabyte, and then deliver them with our hybrid network.  An we can do that in both directions– our customers can bring their giant files to a kiosk for pickup, and within a few hours they can be at any other kiosk in the country, or the world.  We can also offer different grades of service.  For a higher price, we can deliver files to the home– perhaps even using the US postal service.  We can deliver them on the regular schedule, or rush a special order to the customer as soon as it arrives.  The implications of this are significant.  We have customers carrying a large amount of information in a small package.  It could include all the information in their PC, allowing them to use any computer they find rather than carrying one everywhere.  In fact, one might carry all the text one has ever read, accessed through smart data base retrieval software.  Using Zappster, the content of that disk can be backed up on the network, and delivered to the customer anywhere.  Software vendors could install new software for you. Our Blockbuster in a box has evolved to an international network that can deliver any size file, anywhere, at very low cost.
That takes us through the basic description of Zappster.  There are a number of other topics that we have looked at and decided not to discuss in detail today.  For example, we have technology and cost trends for magnetic storage.  We’ve taken a back of the envelope look at system costs, and how we might collect the revenues– we favor an EasyPass type of debit system.  We’ve looked at file security.  And we’re working on some demos.  For example we can demo the ordering process at our website, the download at the kiosk, and playing the content at home or on the road.  We can illustrate PDPs like this that play the content through TVs or laptops, and more elegant ones like this that let you order and even play the content directly.  All these demos are created out of existing pieceparts, of course, but they show that the basic technology is available for a rich set of services. And finally, let’s get back to wireless, because it presents some nice enhancements to our service.
Specifically, we’ve looked at using that high speed Infostation radio for the download from kiosk to PDP, so the customer could be a hundred feet from the kiosk, in a car (maybe even moving).  That would be convenient and add security in some locations.  Radio would also allow multiple access, without plugging in, and access in new types of location, like gas stations and toll booths.  Our research in this area involves both radio design and protocols.  In fact we have significant federal and state grants as the creator and lead institution in this technology.  We think that a practical, low-cost radio can achieve short range download rates almost as high as high as by plugging in.  Still, the radio option is at least 5 years off, and we don’t see it as necessary to the basic concept of Zappster. 
In summary, we have discussed the difficulty of delivering giant files today.
We have shown that video rental is a effective technique, but that Zappster offers powerful advantages.  It is cheaper, serves a mobile population, and can deliver many new forms of content. We have shown how magnetic storage technology is the key to a large menu of content in the kiosk, an almost infinite menu on special order through a hybrid network, and a small, low-cost PDP.
We think the costs look attractive.
Looking out a few more years, we think that Zappster can evolve into a dramatic new type of global network. What is particularly exciting is that we don’t need to invest huge sums for some speculative technology or some undeveloped market, capable of moving giant files anywhere, at low cost. 
Perhaps the most powerful aspect of this is that it is such a practical, low-risk evolution.  It begins with “video rental” an established market that exists today.  It uses a technology that is already acceptable, and who continued improvement is assured.  The evolution to other forms of entertainment and information, and to people on the move, are predictable as well, and personalized advertising provides another large potential opportunity. with a technology that exists as well, and whose evolution is predictable.  The costs are already acceptable, and will continue to improve.  We can end up with a dramatic new form of global system, yet the steps are small and the risk is low.  It seems to us that this is a winning combination.
