A Global Zappster Network

 
The initial description of Zappster has focused on what might be called a “local configuration” (see ZAPPSTER- A Network for the Delivery of Large-File Information).  This includes the Service Center, Hybrid Network, and Kiosks.  The customer, who carries the information (in a personal data pack) to its point of use, provides the final link.

 

The hybrid network provides both physical transport of the information and an Internet capability between the service center and the kiosks.  It has been suggested that the kiosk, with its Internet connection, could also provide limited Internet access for customers at the kiosk.  This connection could logically bypass the Service Center.  An obvious extension of this is that some content may flow to the kiosk directly from the Internet, also bypassing the Service Center.  For example, certain time-sensitive information such as stock quotes could be delivered directly to the kiosks from another source.  Similarly, a particular customer’s personal information could be delivered directly from that person’s home server to a particular kiosk for pickup.  Since this link is also part of the Zappster hybrid network, the load would obviously be controlled.  Regular downloads such as stock quotes would be accounted for in the scheduled system load.  Unscheduled downloads might be size-limited, or could require the kiosk to get permission from the service center.  Where the link is overloaded, the information might have to be routed to the service center, for physical delivery to the kiosk.  The decision process would be handled by the service center, using its automated scheduling software.

 

It is clear from the earlier proposal that a form of hybrid network also supplies the Service Center.  For example, new content may be physically shipped to the Service Center, as in the case of a DVD.  Alternatively, information may arrive at the service center through connection through the more typical communication media, such as fiber optic lines.

 

We now extend this architecture to suggest that the Service centers may be interconnected to form a nationwide or global communications network, which can move information anywhere in an efficient manner.  In general, it will be possible to locate Service Centers where there are major fiber optic capabilities.  In this way, information may move between Service Centers at very low cost, using these very efficient routes.  Kiosks may be used as pickup points as well as endpoints, so that customers may drop off their large files at any kiosk.  These files would be delivered to the Service Center by the hybrid network, and to any other Service Center via the global network.  The global network would utilize both fiber and Physical transport, but we assume that fiber will almost always be used for its speed and low cost.  Physical transport will dominate in the local network, where low-cost fiber connections are not available. 

 

We believe that fiber will become more generally available over time, but that fiber to the home will not be available for many years.  As the fiber network evolves in the local area, its first use will be to fill transportable cashes at satellite locations remote from the service center.  Such caches are discussed in the basic proposal referenced above, but it is assumed there that these satellite caches would be filled through physical transport.  Files would then be taken to the kiosk as the second step in a two-step process.  Using low-cost, high-rate fiber optic delivery from the Service Center to the satellite cache may eventually become possible.  Similarly, fiber may eventually be available to some kiosks as well.  Since the kiosks are located for customer convenience and the satellite caches may be placed where fiber exists, it is more likely that the caches will have fiber connections.  It is important to recognize, however, that such delivery will only be used where the fiber routes represent a cost savings, as along established fiber routes.  Physical transport is very low cost for giant files over the relatively short distances between a Service Centers, satellite caches and kiosks.  This gives Zappster its low cost; and combined with the predicable delivery times provided by the scheduling software, it provides an excellent performance/cost tradeoff.

 

The existence of major fiber connections between Service Centers makes possible multi-level caching strategies, including ones in which some infrequently demanded content is kept in regional or national repositories.

 

Finally, the global Zappster network can provide additional options for delivery and pickup, including home delivery and pickup.  For example, we envision that the postal network could provide this service.  For example, a transportable disk pack in a postal delivery truck could use radio to upload and download to a nearby home.  One can also imagine a PDP in a mailbox, with the postal worker using a plug connection.  For an additional fee, one can even imagine emergency deliveries to kiosk or home between the scheduled mass deliveries.

 

What emerges is a global system which can move giant files relatively quickly, between homes or kiosks, anywhere in the world, with different options available at different price points.
